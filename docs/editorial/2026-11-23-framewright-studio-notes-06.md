Framewright Studio Notes 06 · 23 November 2026

I can tell pretty quickly whether I like the look of a pilot. The more useful question is whether the pilot tells me what to change. For a short explainer, I now try to name the one thing the viewer should be able to see before I spend time on narration, music and the full edit.

## Test the instant the action happens

In [Why NASA Flew Back to Earth to Reach Mars](https://youtu.be/xuO__yvDhHo), the important moment is the engine burn near Earth. I needed the viewer to see the spacecraft pass close to the planet and understand that the engine fires during that pass. The trajectory and timing in the film are schematic.

I made an eleven-second silent pilot in Manim. The ignition was visible in review, and I wanted it to be easier to spot. I added a small circle around the spacecraft and its exhaust during the burn. The circle disappears at shutdown. I also fixed a label that overflowed its panel. The plume and circle shared the same timing, so I could check frames before, during and after the burn to see if they agreed.

This was a small edit with a clear boundary. The circle had a job only while the engine was on. Leaving it in the frame afterwards would have turned it into decoration and weakened the distinction I was trying to show.

## A clear pilot can still need a story

For [The Backup Route](https://youtu.be/lZm5E5-xhLQ), I first made a ten-second OpenMotion diagram. A signal reaches a blocked link, returns to its starting point and takes a second path. The silent sequence was clear in review. I reused that exact animation at its original speed at the end of the finished film.

The edit starts with a real reason to care about detours. When Baltimore's main shipping channel was blocked in 2024, the Port of Virginia handled diverted cargo. The film uses archive footage and an illustrative diagram to show how freight could continue by road or rail. That first half gives the viewer a concrete example before the signal diagram abstracts the idea.

The short pilot answered whether I could follow the signal. The finished edit still needed to tell me why I should follow it.

## Give success its own shot

I tried the same approach on an internal Transit Speed screencast. Both five-second cuts show the same demo click and the same confirmation at the same time. The first shows the full screen with the ordinary pointer. The second moves in on the button and reframes immediately to show the recorded quantity. I found the focused cut clearer in review. I still need to observe whether someone can repeat the action after a single viewing.

For a tutorial I would now write down two things before cutting: which control the viewer should remember, and what on the screen will tell them it worked. Those often need different framings, even when the interface changes only once.

## Keep the test small enough to act on

With Framewright, I put the question and the selected cut in the production record and keep the source and revised clip together. In a normal editor, I can mark the same interval, duplicate the sequence, export both cuts at the same size and write one sentence about what changed. I keep the first export so I can compare it after the correction.

My next useful check is to give someone a fresh copy of the demo and see whether they can complete the action on their first attempt. Until then, the strongest claim I can make is that the button and the confirmation were easier for me to follow. That's already a good reason to try the edit on another lesson.
