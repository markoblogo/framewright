The most useful moment in my Tulvo test came after I changed a shipment’s status. On the board, it was back on hold. In the report I’d saved earlier, it was still released. Both screens looked convincing. Only one reflected the change I had just made.

Tulvo brings an AI assistant, boards, linked records, documents and reports into one workspace. It also presents public document sharing, readership analytics and app hosting on its product site. I focused on two private workflows with fictional records. Tulvo provided Enterprise access for the evaluation, and I have an affiliate relationship with the company. [Tulvo’s platform overview](https://tulvo.io/platform)

## A studio brief becomes a project

I started with a familiar Framewright job. A fictional client asked for a 45-second product explainer with one round of feedback. I logged the inquiry in Tulvo, moved it to *Brief reviewed* and reloaded the page to check that the change had saved.

My first prompt asked for too much and returned a generic plan. A narrower prompt produced a proposal I could work with. I saved it in a private document and used a Kanban board to track the script, first cut and client review. Moving the script to *Done* gave me the visible result I wanted. I still created and checked those steps myself. The test showed how I could organize one job, from a request to a proposal and a task board. [Watch the creative studio test](https://youtu.be/m9lKpzSPnws)

## A shipment needs more than a status

I used our Transit Speed Terminal workflow as a reference and built a separate Tulvo workspace for two fictional freight shipments. Both rows linked to the same company record. One also linked to an authorization record and had a PDF attached. The other had neither. I could open the linked records directly from the shipment list.

Then I tried the same manual edit on both rows. Tulvo let me mark each shipment *Released*, even with the PDF field empty, and both changes survived a reload. That result applies to the list and configuration I tested. It leaves open what a separately configured approval rule can enforce.

The saved report initially counted two released shipments. It marked the empty attachment field as *unverified*. After I moved one shipment back to *Held* and reloaded the report, it still showed the earlier status. I’d use that report as a starting point for a handover and check the shipment record before acting on it. [Watch the freight operations test](https://youtu.be/87jxMAuyOCQ)

## Keep the product demo and the test visible

I made a third video to show how the pieces fit together. It puts our test screens alongside examples from Tulvo’s site, with a source label on each. In one cut, the viewer sees our shipment status change and then Tulvo’s own hosting screen. I didn’t deploy a site or test the public document and analytics features.

I tried that distinction in a nine-second silent edit before making the full overview. During review, I moved the source notes above the images and enlarged the controls people needed to see. That matters in any editor. If the difference between a vendor example and your own result disappears at normal player size, the video can make a stronger claim than the evidence supports. [Watch the Tulvo workspace overview](https://youtu.be/YPYXpOpbCl4)

If I were setting up another workflow, I’d start with one real decision and a few synthetic records. I’d change a source status, reload the board and reopen the saved report. If the process requires a document before a release, I’d test that exact transition with the document present and absent. These checks make it easier to decide which parts of the workspace I can rely on.
