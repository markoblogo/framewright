# The Backup Route

Owner accepted the 34-second English-language YouTube cut and supplied the
[YouTube link](https://youtu.be/lZm5E5-xhLQ) on 23 November 2026. YouTube returned the title “How Cargo Kept Moving After
Baltimore’s Key Bridge Collapse” and channel “ABV — AI · Books · Validation”
for that video ID.

The film opens with the 2024 Key Bridge collapse and the closure of Baltimore’s
main shipping channel. The [Port of Virginia](https://www.portofvirginia.com/news/virginia-completes-fy-2024-with-growth-in-cargo-volumes-and-progress-on-infrastructure-projects/)
handled diverted cargo. [Maersk](https://www.maersk.com/ko-kr/news/articles/2024/03/26/cargo-to-and-from-port-of-baltimore)
described continued service via alternative ports, with onward transport by road
and rail subject to available capacity. The first diagram shows how that service
could work. The closing ten seconds reuse the [approved native OpenMotion animation](../experiments/openmotion-native-export-2026-11-22.md)
at its original speed. The full film was assembled separately at 1920×1080,
30 fps, H.264/AAC, with English narration, an original music bed and separate
English subtitles.

The cover uses a [March 2024 photo of the aftermath](https://www.dvidshub.net/image/8310925/wreckage-key-bridge-collapse)
by David Adams, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Baltimore District. Port b-roll
from [DVIDS](https://www.dvidshub.net/video/279261/port-operations), by Patrick
Bloodgood, is labelled as 2010 archive footage. DVIDS lists both assets as
public domain.

The production tests whether a documented event makes the existing routing
diagram meaningful before it appears. The owner accepted the finished cut.
The original OpenMotion insert was reused, so native export timing and
qualification still refer to the single ten-second test. Audience response
remains unmeasured.
